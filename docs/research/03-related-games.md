# Related games: what each one does and what to take from it

Each entry covers the loop, the economy, how you interact, upgrade paths, **the one idea that makes it different**,
and lessons for us. Entries marked † rely partly on general knowledge of the game rather than on a source pulled for
this research. The comparison matrix is at the end.

---

## Crown of Greed (BLUM Entertainment / PlayWay, March 2026)

The newest serious Majesty successor. Dark European-folklore setting (basilisks, drowners, Baba Yaga) across forests,
swamps, icelands and "deathlands". Steam rating Mixed (about 59–61% of ~700 reviews); critics 70–77.

- **Loop**: Majesty's. Build to attract heroes and citizens; post **contracts** (attack a den, explore a ruin); heroes
  act on their own wills, fears and desires.
- **Subjects**: workers, guards, tax collectors, heroes. Recruitment buildings (e.g. a Hunter's Camp) are
  "headquarters"; the others are economic (marketplace, tavern, trading post, inn, forge, alchemist) or defensive
  (guard towers). Some buildings unlock automatically as the settlement grows.
- **Economy**: gold for everything (construction, upgrades, research, recruitment, spells, contracts). Tax
  collectors carry gold to the castle or guard towers; trading posts trade with other regions. The treasury
  "drains quickly when heroes are swamped".
- **Heroes have random positive and negative traits**, e.g. "a devout warrior, strong against the undead but low on
  health and stamina", or "a rogue with a dark secret or costly vice". Reviewers compare this to Darkest Dungeon.
  Decisions come from **weighted traits rather than fixed rules**.
- **Contract usability**: double-click a building to post an instant defend contract; contracts can be moved and
  adjusted; rewards can be paid early to keep heroes in place.
- **Day/night** makes some enemies and some hero traits stronger.
- **Meta-progression**: scenarios and optional side missions earn **Heritage Points**, spent between missions on
  permanent kingdom upgrades (buildings, economy, defences). Harder difficulties pay more.
- **Criticised**: bugs at launch (heroes stuck, quests not completing, saves failing); heroes **barely develop within
  a mission**; heroes won't attack lairs on their own; personality feels "mechanical" next to Majesty 1; slow hero
  unlocks; no factions; **no random maps or freestyle**; no speed slider; crises aren't pinged on the minimap; no
  visible day/night clock.

**Its one idea**: trait-driven heroes, plus meta-progression between scenarios.
**For us**: traits are the cheapest way to make heroes memorable. Its reviews double as a checklist of what an
indirect-control game must have: random maps, a speed control, crisis pings, visible levelling, and AI that doesn't
get stuck.

## The Kingdom series (Classic 2015, New Lands 2016, Two Crowns 2018, Eighties; published by Raw Fury)

Side-scrolling. You are a monarch on a horse who **cannot fight** (except on certain mounts or with Norse/Greek
weapons in the later settings).

- **One verb: coins.** "Pay" fits coins into slots above a building or object; "Drop" lets them fall (subjects, the
  banker, or the Greed can pick them up). The **purse is physical**: it holds about 40 coins, and coins picked up past
  that spill onto the ground or into the river.
- **Subjects**: recruit **vagrants** at camps with a coin. A villager becomes a worker when it **picks up a tool you
  bought**: bow (2 coins, archer: hunts by day, mans walls at night), hammer (3, builder: builds, repairs, chops,
  works siege weapons), scythe (4–5, farmer), shield (6, knight: squad leader for archers, assaults portals). Vendors
  stock up to four tools. **The job lives in the tool.**
- **Subjects carry coins too** (villager 2, archer 11, farmer 14). Attacks strip them in stages: extra coins first,
  then the tool, then their last coin, which turns them back into a vagrant.
- **Economy**: hunting (archers shoot rabbits and deer), farms on streams (more plots per tier, no income in winter),
  chests in ruins, logging, merchants, and a **banker** who stores coins and pays a little interest (withdraw a third
  at a time; he's only out by day). **Gems** are a second currency for unlocks (mounts, hermits, statues), and they
  never decay.
- **Threat**: the **Greed** come from portals and want **coins, then the crown**. Losing the crown ends the reign. A
  wave arrives every night, released early or late so that it **reaches the walls at midnight** (closer portals send
  it later). **Blood Moons** are big waves on a schedule (Two Crowns: about two days before each season change,
  twice per season from year 3). Destroying portals triggers counter-attacks.
- **Seasons** (16 days each in Two Crowns): winter kills most income (no farming, no game). Plan your savings.
- **Upgrade paths**: the **town centre** goes through 6–7 tiers (campfire → tents → wooden fortifications → … →
  castle keep), each unlocking vendors (bows and hammers at the campfire, scythes at wooden fortifications, shields at
  the keep). **Walls** sit on fixed mounds and go up in tiers (wood → stone → iron; birch and pine in Norse Lands at
  half strength). **Hermits** found in the wild give a tower a whole new function (ballista, fire tower, stable,
  bakery…). **Statues** give lasting blessings (archery, building, farming).
- **Persistence**: islands remain in Two Crowns and **decay** while you're away: walls crumble outer to inner, then
  subjects lose their tools. Losing the crown passes rule to an **heir**, with extra decay.
- **Interaction design**: no HUD and few words. Everything is learnt by **seeing coin slots appear** over things. Two
  buttons on any platform, including mobile.

**Its one idea**: the monarch is the cursor. You must physically ride to a place to spend money there, and your
reach is your time.
**For us**: tool-defines-role is a lovely indirect lever. A coin purse you carry makes spending spatial. Waves timed
to hit at midnight, Blood Moon schedules and winter famine all suit a day/night build/wave game like ours.

## Dungeon Keeper 1 / 2 (Bullfrog 1997 / 1999); War for the Overworld † (2015) is a spiritual successor

You're an evil keeper building a dungeon underground, defending a Dungeon Heart (lose when it's destroyed).

- **Imps** are free workers that dig, claim, haul and fortify. Never tired, never paid; create more with a spell.
- **Creatures arrive through a Portal** only if **your rooms attract them** (each species needs particular rooms and
  room sizes, e.g. a Bile Demon wants a 5×5 Hatchery plus a Workshop), there's **Lair space**, and some remain in the
  level's creature pool. Your build decides your roster, Majesty-style.
- **Needs**: a Lair (sleep and heal), the Hatchery (eat chickens), **wages on payday** (every ~8 minutes; wage
  grows 35% of base per level, so a level-10 creature costs 4.15× its level-1 wage), and work they like (the
  Training Room for fighters, the Library for "thinkers", the Workshop for trolls). Unhappy creatures sulk, vandalise,
  fight each other, **leave**, or (DK2) **rebel and join the heroes**. Natural enemies fight in shared lairs.
- **Rooms**: Treasure Room (50 a tile; capacity), Lair, Hatchery, Training Room (the biggest gold sink, since
  training costs money), Library (research spells and rooms), Workshop (traps and doors), Prison, Torture Chamber
  (convert enemies; torturing your own speeds up their kind), Temple (pray, sacrifice), Guard Post, Graveyard,
  Scavenger Room (lure other keepers' creatures), Casino (DK2: **a payout lever** that either wins back wage gold or
  makes creatures happier). **Room efficiency** rewards compact, walled-in rooms.
- **The Hand of Evil**: pick up creatures and drop them into a room (that assigns the job) or onto intruders (that
  starts a fight); pick up gold; **slap** a creature to make it work faster, which annoys it; drop gold on an unhappy
  creature to cheer it up instantly. Creatures dislike being held.
- **Possession**: take over one creature and play it in first person. It fights, digs and explores faster, but can't
  do jobs. **Call to Arms** rallies everyone to a spot. Keeper spells cost gold (DK1) or mana (DK2).
- **Thought bubbles** over creatures show what they want (food, pay, sleep).

**Its one idea**: you manage a workforce of selfish employees with needs and wages, and you have a hand that can
pick them up.
**For us**: wages and **payday** are a strong way to make a big roster cost something (Majesty has no upkeep). Rooms
that attract species, thought bubbles, and drop-into-room assignment are all proven. A costly override (slap or
possess) balances indirect control.

## Black & White (Lionhead 2001)

A god game. You're a disembodied **hand**.

- **Belief** is the currency of control: villages believe in you because of **impressive acts seen by villagers**. More
  witnesses and rarer acts give more belief, and repeating an act wears off. Belief and population set your
  **influence**: a circle where your hand works. Outside it you can only act for a short while.
- **Prayer power** comes from worshippers at the worship site (how many you send is set by a totem, 0–100% of the
  village) and pays for **miracles** (food, wood, water, heal, lightning, shield). Worshippers don't work, so you trade
  labour for magic.
- **The Creature**: a giant pet that learns by **reinforcement**. Stroke or slap it after it does something, with a
  strength you choose. **Leashes**: Learning (it copies what you do), Aggression and Compassion (it acts that way while
  leashed). Players advise 10–30% rewards, because 100% every time stops it telling better from worse. The
  most-cited flaw: **you're never sure which action you rewarded**.

**Its one idea**: you teach an AI agent your values by rewarding and punishing it.
**For us**: two cautionary lessons. Indirect control only works if the player can tell **what caused what**. And
"impressiveness wears off with repetition" is a neat economy rule for any reputation or favour currency.

## Thronefall (Grizzly Games; early access 2023)

A minimalist build-by-day, defend-by-night game; critics recommend it at about 95% (OpenCritic). It's the most
**phone-shaped** game here, closest to Hold the Hall.

- **Day**: ride your king around **preset build spots** and spend the **gold paid out each morning** (only buildings
  that survived the night pay). Houses and mills make gold, towers defend, barracks and archery ranges make units.
- **Upgrades**: buildings upgrade in steps, with **a fork at some steps between two options**. A barracks or range is
  **locked to the unit type you choose**. The castle centre unlocks more.
- **Night**: waves come down known lanes, and **you know which enemies are coming** (with one exception). Units
  fight on their own. You **walk up to a group and have it follow you** (an upgrade lets followers attack while
  following); at dawn they go back to their building.
- **The king fights** directly, with weapons unlocked through the game (bow, spear, sword).
- **Meta**: a small set of hand-made maps with fixed blueprints (4 in early access, about 10 later), levelling that unlocks perks, weapons and "mutators" (harder
  modifiers for more score).
- **Criticised**: fixed layouts limit replay; difficulty spikes; enemies sometimes slip round walls.

**Its one idea**: radically cut-down RTS: preset slots, one income moment a day, units that mostly fight on their own,
and one hero you steer.
**For us**: "gold at dawn from survivors" is a great rule (losing buildings hurts tomorrow). Fork upgrades fit
our 5-stage upgrades. "Follow the king" is the lightest possible direct control, and it suits phones.

## Dungeon Village / Dungeon Village 2 (Kairosoft; Japan 2011, Android and iOS 2012; Steam and consoles later)

Mobile-native and the closest **commercial phone version of Majesty's shop loop**.

- **Adventurers act alone**: they fight monsters for a while (how long depends on their *Work* stat), then come to
  town to shop, eat and sleep. Downed adventurers recover over time or get **carried to the Inn by comrades**.
- **You build the town**: shops, eateries, inns, houses, plants and paths. **Buildings level up through use** (visited
  enough times, then remodelled by you, up to level 5), and items raise a building's price, quality and appeal. **Area
  boosts**: decorations raise neighbours' price and quality, buildings raise their appeal. **Upkeep** on every
  building means overbuilding loses money.
- **Quests**: up to 9 adventurers **volunteer at random** for a quest, and you can pay to fill the gaps. **Bosses**
  roam the town and cost popularity until killed.
- **Popularity** brings new adventurers, events, monsters and **medals** (one per 200 popularity). Medals raise an
  adventurer's Satisfaction and Work, and unlock **job changes**. A job mastered at level 10 gives permanent stats
  and unlocks that job for everyone, and carries to a new game.

**Its one idea**: the shop economy on a phone: adventurers are customers, and buildings level up from being used.
**For us**: proof that Majesty's loop works in short phone sessions. "Buildings level from use", area adjacency
bonuses and upkeep give town layout a puzzle our walls-first design lacks.

## Against the Storm (Eremite Games / Hooded Horse; 1.0 2023)

A "roguelite city builder". Each settlement is a run of about an hour or two.

- **Win/lose race**: fill **Reputation** (for example 18 points) before the Queen's **Impatience** fills (lose at 14).
  Reputation comes from **orders** (objectives), **Resolve** (species happiness above a threshold pays out reputation
  over time), glade events and caches.
- **Year cycle**: Drizzle → Clearance → **Storm** (together about 8 minutes before the storm). Farms sow in Drizzle and
  harvest in Clearance.
- **Hostility** rises with years passed, **glades you open** and villagers you add, and is reduced by hearths and
  lost villagers. **Your expansion is your threat**, as with Majesty's sewers.
- **Glades**: open fog patches. Small ones are cheap resources (+~15 hostility). Dangerous ones (~50% chance of a ruin,
  a timed event: 13 min threat, 5–7.5 min to solve). Forbidden ones are far away, rich, and sometimes lethal.
- **Species** have different needs (housing, complex food, clothing, services). **Cornerstones** are run-wide perk
  picks.
- **Meta**: the Citadel's upgrades between runs; difficulty up to Prestige 20; a long-term goal (the Adamantine Seal).

**Its one idea**: every run is a race between two meters, and pushing outwards always costs danger.
**For us**: two meters (progress against pressure) give runs a clear end. "Opening fog raises hostility" is the
cleanest version of "exploration has a price", and it suits explore bounties.

## RimWorld (Ludeon 2018)

A colony sim driven by an **AI storyteller**.

- **Storytellers** (Cassandra: rising tension with breathers; Phoebe: long gaps; Randy: random) choose events.
  Difficulty is set separately.
- **Raid points** decide raid size from **colony wealth** (buildings count at half), colonist count, combat animals,
  difficulty and an **adaptation** factor (rises over time, drops when colonists die or go down). Minimum 35, maximum
  10,000 points. So **getting rich gets you raided harder**, and "wealth management" is a strategy.
- **Work priorities**: each colonist gets jobs ranked 1–4 (or simple checkboxes). Colonists do all priority-1 work
  before priority 2, with no regard for travel time (hauling at 1 means hauling everything first).
- **Drafting**: in combat you **draft** colonists, giving direct RTS control (move, line up, target). They ignore their
  needs while drafted, and drafting ends after a while with no threat.
- **Mood**: 0–100% from stacked thoughts ("moodlets"). Below three thresholds come minor, major and extreme mental
  breaks (hiding, binges, sabotage, even murder). **Traits** (1–3 per colonist) change needs, moods and what they'll do.

**Its one idea**: autonomous agents by default, with a **temporary switch to full control** for combat; a director
that scales threat to your wealth.
**For us**: drafting is the standard answer to "indirect control is frustrating in a crisis". Wealth-to-raid-points
is a formula we could reuse for night raids: wave size from treasury plus building value, not just the wave number.

## Stronghold (Firefly 2001) †

A castle builder with RTS sieges.

- **Popularity** (−100 … +100) is the key meter: above zero, peasants arrive at the campfire; below, they leave. It's
  set by **taxes** (bribes ↔ high tax), **food rations and variety**, **ale** (inns), **religion** (chapels,
  priests), and the **fear factor**: "good things" (gardens, maypoles) make workers happy but slower, "bad things"
  (gibbets, stocks) make them faster but scared.
- **Production chains**: wood, stone and iron; farms → granary; weapons from workshops → armoury → troops.
- Combat units are directly controlled. The economy is indirect (peasants staff buildings themselves).

**Its one idea**: one popularity meter, made of a few understandable levers, sets your workforce.
**For us**: a happiness or popularity meter made of explicit levers (taxes, food, ale, faith, fear) could be the
peasants' side of our town while heroes are the military side. The tax-rate slider pulls directly against hero
loyalty.

## Kingdom Rush (Ironhide 2011; Frontiers, Origins, Vengeance, Alliance) †

The benchmark tower defence on phones.

- **Towers** (archer, barracks, mage, artillery) on **fixed plots** upgrade three times, then **branch into one of two
  specialisations** (KR1: Rangers or Musketeers for archers, Holy Order or Barbarian Hall for barracks…).
- **Barracks have a rally point**: you set where their soldiers stand.
- **Heroes** (from KR1 on mobile, then every game): one unit you move directly, with XP and levels within a stage and
  a respawn timer. In later games heroes have persistent skill points.
- **Two global spells** on cooldowns (rain of fire, reinforcements).
- **Meta**: stars (3 / 2 / 1 for lives left: 18–20 / 6–17 / 1–5) buy permanent tower and spell upgrades.
- **Readability**: enemy paths are fixed and shown; new enemies introduced one by one with a card; early-wave call
  buttons pay bonus gold.

**Its one idea**: the most polished phone TD: few, legible choices with a strong level-4 fork.
**For us**: the HUD and controls bar to clear on phones. Rally points are a gentle form of area control. The
early-wave bonus is a good risk-for-gold choice.

## Darkest Dungeon (Red Hook 2016) †

A roster game about **expendable heroes**.

- **Hamlet** buildings (stagecoach, tavern, abbey, sanitarium, guild, blacksmith, survivalist) are upgraded with
  **heirlooms** looted from dungeons. Each building always wants the same kinds, in growing amounts.
- **Stress**: heroes gain stress in dungeons. At 100 their resolve is tested: usually an **affliction** (selfish,
  paranoid…), sometimes a virtue. At 200 they can suffer a heart attack. Stress relief costs gold and town time.
- **Quirks**: positive and negative traits that come and go, removable or lockable for a price.
- **The stagecoach** brings new recruits. Death is permanent, and the game expects you to lose heroes.

**Its one idea**: heroes as a churned, flawed roster, and the town as their maintenance shop.
**For us**: town buildings as **hero maintenance** (cure, rest, retrain) and named, flawed heroes you're attached to
but can lose. That's Majesty's graveyard and resurrection economy from the other side.

## Shorter notes †

| Game | The relevant idea |
| ---- | ----------------- |
| **They Are Billions** (2019) | Colony against huge zombie hordes; walls and chokepoints decide it; one breach cascades. A final wave on a clock. Very close to our wall-first pillar |
| **Dwarf Fortress** (2006–) | Pure designation control: mark dig, chop, build; dwarves pick up jobs by labour settings; squads take orders. Needs and moods cause "tantrum spirals". The extreme of emergent story |
| **The Settlers** series | Economy-only control: build and road; settlers carry goods themselves. Attacks are ordered by picking a building and a number of soldiers. Logistics is the game |
| **Tropico** series | Citizens autonomous; you set **edicts**, build, set wages and rents; factions react; elections. Policy as control |
| **Northgard** (2018) | Seasons with harsh winters; happiness from food and comfort; one zone colonised at a time; clans with distinct bonuses; several win conditions (fame, lore, trade, war) |
| **Battle Brothers** (2017) | A mercenary roster with **daily wages and food**; morale; permanent injuries. Upkeep makes every hire a commitment |
| **War for the Overworld** (2015) | Dungeon Keeper rebuilt, the same hand, rooms and payday |
| **Dungeons** series (Kalypso) | Dungeon Keeper inverted: heroes invade and you "please" them (gold, items) before killing them; heroes as a resource to farm |

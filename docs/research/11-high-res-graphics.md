# Fix the edges before adding pixels

The browser games and web 3D pieces admired for their graphics do not look high-resolution because they ship huge textures. They move expensive work offline (baked lighting, palette textures, prefiltered maps), keep text and UI in the DOM, cap or adapt the render resolution, and ship GPU-compressed textures (KTX2/Basis) so that detail fits in phone memory. Hold the Hall already has the first two habits: its battlefield is pre-baked inked sprites and its HUD is DOM. Its softness on phones comes from elsewhere. The sprite edges are hard alpha-test cuts that the existing MSAA cannot smooth. The atlas has 3 px of padding with no edge-colour bleed, so mips fringe when zoomed out. Units are baked at only 56 pixels per world unit (ppu) and are magnified from zoom 1.55 on a Pixel 7. The painted ground is magnified up to 4× at max zoom. The memory picture is the binding constraint: the standard set alone is about **272 MiB of uncompressed GPU textures**, and HD is about **751 MiB**, both in the range where iOS Safari kills tabs. The highest-value plan is therefore staged. First, do the near-free fixes: alpha-to-coverage, edge bleed and padding, dropping the icon pages from the GPU, and a per-terrain ground detail. Second, cut texture memory about 4× with KTX2, which in turn pays for sharper units and eventually for HD sprites near the camera on phones. A third @3x set, a global rise in ground resolution and a WebGPU port are poor value for this goal. Every on-device number below is still arithmetic, not measurement, so the plan opens with a measurement pass.

> **Update, 20 October 2026.** Since this report was written, sharper soldiers and heroes (80 / 112 px a tile in HD)
> grew the atlases: uncompressed with mips, standard is now ~325 MiB and HD ~1,010 MiB. Stage 1 is done (see
> ROADMAP): alpha-to-coverage on High, icon pages off the GPU (~5 / ~50 MiB), anisotropy trimmed, and exact
> device-pixel canvases. Why live 3D models were rejected is measured in [12-live-3d-models.md](12-live-3d-models.md).

## Admired web games buy their polish offline and keep the runtime lean

The pattern across case studies is consistent: the "expensive" look is produced before the game runs. Bruno Simon's folio-2025 sets a **palette texture** directly on three.js materials, compresses every texture embedded in its GLBs with Basis **ETC1S at quality 255**, converts UI images to WebP, and keeps the uncompressed originals so it can re-run compression with different settings ([folio-2025 README](https://github.com/brunosimon/folio-2025)). Much of its life comes from cheap procedural world systems such as wind, grass, weather, day cycles and snow rather than heavy assets (same source). His "My Room in 3D" blends pre-baked lighting textures in a shader instead of using real-time lights ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20210913-my-room-in-3d)), and Three.js Journey teaches baking a Blender render into textures as the core workflow ([Three.js Journey](https://threejs-journey.com/lessons/baking-and-exporting-the-scene)). PlayCanvas and Arm's Seemore set out to deliver "console quality graphics on mobile" in the browser. They used prefiltered and box-projected cubemaps, dual baked AO maps interpolated at runtime, and fake translucency ([The Making of Seemore](https://blog.playcanvas.com/the-making-of-seemore-webgl)). The 2019 rebuild added HDR lightmaps, interpolated pre-baked shadow maps, batching, GPU-driven animation and **ETC2 textures**, and its asynchronous streaming loaded about 5× faster than the original ([PlayCanvas](https://blog.playcanvas.com/arm-and-playcanvas-open-source-seemore-webgl-demo)). Shopify's Spring '26 Edition splits the work the same way: the DOM carries content and interaction, while one WebGL canvas supplies atmosphere, including video preprocessed into **KTX2 array textures** ([Codrops](https://tympanus.net/codrops/?p=116711)).

For 2D and sprite games the standard answer to "crisp at any zoom" is multiple atlas resolutions. PixiJS expands patterns such as `bunny@{0.5,1,2}x` and picks the variant at runtime by format support and device pixel ratio ([PixiJS resolver guide](https://pixijs.com/8.x/guides/components/assets/resolver)). Packers such as FastPack export @1x and @2x from one source and record the scale in metadata ([FastPack](https://www.mintlify.com/Hexeption/FastPack/packing/multi-resolution)). Kingdom Rush, the obvious genre reference, began as a Flash game on Armor Games in July 2011 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_Rush)), so its art was rasterised from vectors at runtime. No primary Ironhide source explains how that art was converted for HTML5 and mobile. The case studies also have honest gaps: Krunker, Hordes.io, Venge.io, Shell Shockers, Lusion, Active Theory and Abeto's Messenger have no published technical write-ups, and no source describes a pre-baked isometric web game shipping KTX2 sprite atlases. The lesson that transfers is the discipline, not any one trick. Hold the Hall's bake-time ink and VSM shadows already are its "baked lighting", and its DOM HUD already renders at device resolution whatever the WebGL scale.

Practitioner budgets for phones converge. Common advice is about **100 draw calls and 100k vertices**, textures at 2048 px or below, DPR capped at `Math.min(devicePixelRatio, 2)`, and adaptive DPR with a flip-flop limit ([Utsubo](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-best-practices-100-tips)). drei's PerformanceMonitor averages over 250 ms, holds a dead band of [50, 60] fps on 60 Hz screens and steps in 0.1 increments ([drei docs](https://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/performances/performance-monitor)). PlayCanvas exposes a runtime `maxPixelRatio` chosen by device tier ([PlayCanvas manual](https://developer.playcanvas.com/user-manual/optimization/runtime-devicepixelratio/)), and its users report UI text going blurry below a ratio of about 2 on phones ([PlayCanvas forum](https://forum.playcanvas.com/t/solved-ui-is-blurry-on-mobile/12063)). That is exactly the problem a DOM HUD avoids. Hold the Hall already has a frame-rate-driven `DynamicResolution` with 0.1 steps and a ceiling after a failed step up (`src/render/dynamicResolution.js`), so it needs no new controller.

## Phone budgets are set by texture memory, then fill, not download

Download size flatters image formats. WebP, PNG and JPEG are all decoded to **4 bytes per texel on the GPU** ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/webp-vs-ktx2-for-web-textures/58651)), and a full mip chain adds a third, so a 4096² texture costs about 90 MB whatever its file size ([Don McCurdy](https://www.donmccurdy.com/2024/02/11/web-texture-formats/)). Hold the Hall's WebP pages are unusually small at 0.55 to 0.88 bits per texel, because they are mostly flat inked colour and transparent space. That makes the gap between download and memory extreme. The standard set downloads in **6.8 MB** but occupies about 272 MiB with mips. HD downloads in **11.1 MB** and occupies about 751 MiB. All pages are uploaded as uncompressed RGBA8 with GPU-generated mips (`src/render/spriteAssets.js:58-61`).

Those numbers sit inside the danger zone that iOS reports describe, though no hard limit exists. WebKit bug 300782 (October 2025, still NEW) describes texture-heavy pages being killed and reloaded under memory pressure, sometimes in a loop ending in "A problem repeatedly occurred". No OOM error reaches JavaScript and no `webglcontextlost` fires first, and a WebKit engineer gave **no numeric budget** ([WebKit bug 300782](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=300782)). Earlier cases put a crash at about 900 MB of WebGL memory ([WebKit 190518](https://www2.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=190518)). Unity developers report reloads past about 256 MB, and one advises a 400 MiB target because 2 to 3 GiB iPhones crash above roughly 500 MiB ([Unity forum](https://discussions.unity.com/t/webgl-ios-unity-sometimes-reloads-after-startup/1541942); [Unity forum](https://discussions.unity.com/t/webgl-build-setting-unity-6/1541471)). CrazyGames disables Unity games on iOS by default "due to memory-related crashes" ([CrazyGames requirements](https://docs.crazygames.com/requirements/technical/)). Hold the Hall's default phone configuration (standard sprites on High) totals roughly 272 MiB of sprites, 33 MiB of ground and about 19 MB of framebuffer, possibly ×4 under MSAA. That is around 330 MiB or more before the browser's own copies, so it is already borderline on older iPhones. The game's context-loss handler steps the preset down (`src/game/Game.js:34-47`), but presets do not change the sprite set, and an iOS kill fires no event at all. **HD on a phone is not viable while textures are uncompressed.**

GPU compression is the standard fix, and support on phones is effectively universal. ASTC reaches **99.73% of Android and 99.99% of iOS** WebGL2 reports, while Windows relies on BC7 at 99.69% ([web3dsurvey ASTC](https://web3dsurvey.com/webgl2/extensions/WEBGL_compressed_texture_astc); [web3dsurvey BPTC](https://web3dsurvey.com/webgl2/extensions/EXT_texture_compression_bptc)). Khronos's own example went from 96 MB to **21 MB of GPU memory** after moving from JPG+PNG to KTX2 ([KTX Artist Guide](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/3D-Formats-Guidelines/blob/main/KTXArtistGuide.md)), and PlayCanvas reports a 6× VRAM cut ([PlayCanvas blog](https://blog.playcanvas.com/basis-texture-compression-arrives-in-playcanvas)). The two Basis modes trade differently. ETC1S runs at about 0.3 to 3 bpp on disk and suits flat-colour and interface art, but "doesn't produce quality results on complex textures" and struggles with saturated neighbouring colours. UASTC is 8 bpp before Zstd, near BC7 quality, and Khronos moved a high-variation texture with alpha from ETC1S to UASTC after review (same guide; [basis_universal](https://github.com/BinomialLLC/basis_universal)). One subtlety matters for this game. With alpha, ETC1S transcodes to an 8 bpp format (ETC2 RGBA) on phones, the same GPU cost as UASTC to ASTC 4×4 ([three.js KTX2Loader](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/jsm/loaders/KTX2Loader.js)). **ETC1S saves download, not phone memory.**

| GPU format | bpp | One 2048² page + mips | Standard set (13 pages) | HD set (36 pages) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RGBA8 (today, from WebP) | 32 | 21.3 MiB | ≈272 MiB | ≈751 MiB |
| ASTC 4×4 / BC7 / ETC2 RGBA | 8 | 5.3 MiB | ≈68 MiB | ≈188 MiB |
| ASTC 6×6 | 3.56 | 2.4 MiB | ≈31 MiB | ≈85 MiB |

*Arithmetic from the repo's page texel counts and the bpp figures in the [basis_universal README](https://github.com/BinomialLLC/basis_universal).*

The catch is download. UASTC with RDO and Zstd lands at about 4 to 6 bpp on disk, which the audit estimates at **27 to 40 MB for the standard set and 74 to 111 MB for HD**, against today's 6.8 and 11.1 MB. ETC1S at 1 to 2 bpp keeps the standard set near 7 to 13 MB. Portal ceilings make this concrete. CrazyGames allows at most **20 MB of initial download for mobile homepage eligibility** and 50 MB in general ([CrazyGames](https://docs.crazygames.com/requirements/technical/)). Poki publishes no number and only asks developers to keep files "as small as possible" ([Poki](https://sdk.poki.com/deals)), and a Poki engineer's 2018 data tied a 10 MB download to about 0.8 conversion to play ([W3C slides](https://www.w3.org/2018/12/games-workshop/slides/12-size-matters.pdf)). Basis Universal v2.5 adds XUASTC at roughly 2.25 bpp, which could be the middle path, but its support in three.js's transcoder is unverified ([basis_universal](https://github.com/BinomialLLC/basis_universal)). KTX2 brings two more benefits: transcoding runs in workers and uploads are 4 to 8× faster ([Don McCurdy](https://www.donmccurdy.com/2024/02/11/web-texture-formats/)), whereas decoded images upload on the main thread and can freeze for up to about a second per large image ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/loader-textureloader-imagebitmaploader-blocks-js-main-thread-causing-temporary-freeze/44579)).

Fill is the second budget. Pixel count rises with the square of DPR, so native on a 2.6 to 3× phone is 7 to 9 times the fragment work of DPR 1. One three.js user saw 40 fps on iOS with a single mesh at native DPR and got 60 fps back by removing `setPixelRatio` ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/low-fps-on-ios-mobile-with-pixel-ration-set-as-window-devicepixelratio/4963)). MSAA, by contrast, is cheap on tile-based phone GPUs because samples resolve on chip. PowerVR calls 2× "virtually free" and says 4× "will noticeably impact performance" through smaller tiles and per-sample blending ([Imagination](https://docs.imgtec.com/performance-guides/graphics-recommendations/html/topics/msaa-performance.html)). Qualcomm resolves in GMEM during the tile store ([Qualcomm](https://developer.qualcomm.com/software/snapdragon-profiler/app-notes/anti-aliasing-with-adreno)), and Meta calls 4× "extremely cheap" on its Arm-based headsets ([Meta](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/native/android/gpu-improved-algorithms/)). Extra full-screen passes are the costly thing on tilers. Arm estimates a **+400% bandwidth penalty** for rebinding a render target mid-frame and warns against storing MSAA data to DRAM ([Arm](https://developer.arm.com/community/arm-community-blogs/b/mobile-graphics-and-gaming-blog/posts/mali-performance-2-how-to-correctly-handle-framebuffers); [Arm, Vulkanised 2025](https://www.vulkan.org/user/pages/09.events/vulkanised-2025/T18-Pete-Harris-Arm-MobileBestPractices.pdf)). This favours MSAA plus shader-level fixes over post-processing.

## Hold the Hall loses sharpness at edges, units and ground, not resolution

The audit's central table compares canvas pixels with baked sprite pixels. A Pixel 7 at High (native DPR 2.625) shows about 36 canvas pixels per view unit at zoom 1 and 130 at the maximum zoom of 3.6. Against that, standard sprites carry 72 ppu, units 56 and elites 43, while HD carries 144. The ground carries 32 texels per tile horizontally and 52 vertically. Above 1 in the table, the image is magnified and soft.

| Zoom (Pixel 7, High) | Standard 72 | Units 56 | Elites 43 | HD 144 | Ground (h / v) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.327 (far minimum) | 0.16 | 0.21 | 0.27 | 0.08 | 0.37 / 0.23 |
| 1 (start) | 0.50 | 0.64 | 0.84 | 0.25 | 1.13 / 0.69 |
| 2 | 1.00 | 1.29 | 1.68 | 0.50 | 2.25 / 1.39 |
| 3.6 (max) | **1.80** | **2.32** | **3.0** | 0.90 | **4.06 / 2.50** |

*Screen pixels per sprite pixel, computed from `src/render/Renderer.js` view constants and `public/sprites*/atlas.json`.*

Three conclusions follow. First, **units are the blurriest thing on screen when zoomed in**: they are the most magnified group, and they also take 44% of standard-set texels. Second, the repo's documented claim that "a phone shows fewer pixels per tile than the standard set has, even zoomed in" (`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:92-93`) holds only when the pixel ratio is at or below about 1.46. At High it is false on every current phone. Third, at zoom 1 and below, where most play happens, sprites are minified, not magnified. Sharpness there depends on edges and mips, not on bake resolution.

Edges are the most visible loss and the cheapest to fix. Object sprites use `alphaTest: 0.5` with depth writes (`src/render/spriteLayer.js:113`), so every silhouette and ink line is cut hard at screen resolution. High enables MSAA (`antialias: true`), but MSAA smooths triangle edges, and a sprite quad's triangle edges lie in transparent texels, so High pays for MSAA and gets almost nothing from it on the battlefield. Alpha-to-coverage is the standard cure for alpha-tested foliage and cutouts: it turns alpha into a sub-pixel coverage mask, giving 5 levels at 4× MSAA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_to_coverage)). three.js supports it with one flag, `Material.alphaToCoverage`, which works only with an MSAA context ([three.js Material docs](https://neofixer.arizona.edu/css/CSSOrbit/asteroidJS/three/docs/api/en/materials/Material.html)). With `alphaTest` set, the local three.js shader applies the fwidth-sharpened variant that Ben Golus describes and Godot shipped as "alpha antialiasing" ([Godot PR #40364](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/pull/40364)). Nothing in `src/` uses it today.

Zoomed out, the atlas itself degrades. Pages have 3 px of padding with no edge extrusion (`src/render/atlasPacking.js:27`) and straight, non-premultiplied alpha with GPU-generated mips. At mip level 2 (4:1 minification) that padding falls below one texel. That happens at about zoom 0.5 for standard sprites on phones, and at the start zoom for HD on a 1080p desktop. Packers and engines agree that filtering straight alpha leaks dark colour into edges, and that the fixes are extrusion (1 px for bilinear, more for mips) or filling transparent texels with the border colour ([FastPack](https://www.mintlify.com/Hexeption/FastPack/packing/extrude); [TexturePacker options](https://pytexturepacker.readthedocs.io/en/master/2_options.html)). Unity rounds compressed-atlas padding up to multiples of 4 for block alignment ([Unity docs](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/script-reference/unityengine/texture2d/packtextures)). The code predicts dark fringes, neighbour bleed and thin spears and flagpoles dropping out under the 0.5 cut, but nobody has screenshotted it on a device yet.

The rest of the loss is spread thinly. The ground is a single `CanvasTexture` at 32 px per tile (2560² on a Medium map), and a generic grass-stroke detail texture fades in between zoom 1.3 and 2.4 (`src/render/groundDetail.js`), with nothing for sand, gravel or forest floor. Raising `GROUND_PX` globally runs into texture-size limits: 64 px per tile on a Medium map needs 5120², and only 75% of Android WebGL2 reports support 8192 ([web3dsurvey](https://web3dsurvey.com/webgl2/parameters/MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE)). Low and Medium start touch devices at a render scale of 0.75 and let the browser stretch the canvas about 3.5×. That is a whole-image blur no atlas can fix. A fractional DPR is a smaller suspect: three.js floors the canvas width (412 × 2.625 = 1081.5 becomes 1081). WebGL Fundamentals says only `ResizeObserver` with `devicePixelContentBoxSize` gives an exact device-pixel canvas, and that rounding at fractional DPRs is "undefined by any specs" ([WebGL Fundamentals](https://webglfundamentals.org/webgl/lessons/webgl-resizing-the-canvas.html)). Memory has its own waste. The UI-icon pages (standard page 12, HD pages 32 to 35) are uploaded to the GPU on the first frame because `SpriteLayer` makes a material for every page, even though the battlefield never draws them. That is about **16 MiB in standard and 68 MiB in HD, with mips**. Two smaller oddities round it out. Anisotropy 4 on camera-facing sprite quads does no useful work. And because ink outlines are a fixed 3 bake pixels, HD lines come out about a third thinner in world units than standard ones, so the two sets do not look identical.

The CSS colour grade (`sepia(0.08) saturate(1.04) contrast(1.04)` on High, `src/render/Renderer.js:49-56`) is colour-only and does not blur. Its cost is uncertain. Chromium applies canvas filters in the GPU compositor ([Chromium design doc](https://www.chromium.org/developers/design-documents/image-filters/)), which plausibly means an extra full-device-resolution filter pass each frame. The only evidence that this hurts on mobile is from 2016 ([A Faster Web](https://www.afasterweb.com/2016/01/31/css-filters-and-mobile-performance/)), so it needs measuring before anyone moves the grade into a shader.

## The cheapest sharpness comes from AA and atlas hygiene; KTX2 unlocks the rest

Ranked by visible gain per unit of risk, the options sort into three groups.

**Near-free wins.** Alpha-to-coverage on object sprites is a one-line change in `SpriteLayer._makePage`. It anti-aliases every silhouette and ink line, and on tilers it costs close to nothing because MSAA already resolves on chip. Its limit is that it only works where `antialias: true`, which today means High only. Medium could be given 2× MSAA, which PowerVR calls virtually free, so that it benefits too. Edge-colour bleed into transparent texels plus padding of about 8 px, aligned to 4-pixel blocks, removes zoomed-out fringes. It also prepares the atlas for block compression, at a cost of perhaps 2 to 4% more atlas area. Because packing is kept out of the bake fingerprint, this is a repack, not a full re-render (`src/render/atlasPacking.js:3-8`). Dropping icon pages from the GPU saves 16 to 68 MiB with no visual cost. The HUD must keep reading icons from the decoded images (`src/render/spriteBaker.js:318`).

**The structural lever.** KTX2 is the only change that makes room for more pixels on phones. The loader and transcoder already ship with three.js (`basis_transcoder.wasm` is 527 KB). Encoding must start from the lossless cached sprites, not the WebP, as Basis itself advises ([Basis README mirror](https://skia.googlesource.com/external/github.com/BinomialLLC/basis_universal/+/8bf012367213fb3668beadf2aa071d0d01443785)). The mips must ship inside the file, because compressed textures cannot generate mips at runtime ([KTX2Loader](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/jsm/loaders/KTX2Loader.js)). The main quality risk is the alpha cut: the repo's own AVIF trial found that lossy alpha flipped about 0.1% of edge pixels across the 0.5 threshold (`docs/ROADMAP.md:80-83`), and block compression can do the same. The likely compromise is a per-page mix: UASTC for the close-up pages (walls, buildings, units) and ETC1S for the rest, or UASTC on desktop HD only. A trial encode decides it. Once memory drops about 4×, raising units from 56 to 72 ppu (elites from 43 to 56) costs about 17 MiB instead of 70 MiB, and makes units sharp up to zoom 2 on phones.

**Larger bets with conditional value.** Streaming HD pages by zoom on phones would mean always loading standard, fetching HD pages for the groups in view past about zoom 1.8 to 2.0, and evicting them on zoom-out. That brings HD sharpness to the zoom levels where it shows, but it takes about a week. It also risks upload stalls and a visible pop, and because the atlas is grouped by kind, a walled base may still need most wall pages. A contrast-adaptive sharpening (RCAS/CAS) pass would crispen Low and Medium on phones, folding in the colour grade for free. three.js's official `FSR1Node` targets the TSL/WebGPU pipeline, and its docs say to use it only when fragment-bound ([three.js FSR1Node](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/FSR1Node.html)). A port of RCAS's 5-tap cross to plain GLSL is therefore the realistic route, at the price of one extra full-screen pass, which Arm's bandwidth warnings make worth measuring first. A per-terrain ground detail splat (grass strokes, gravel, sand ripples, forest litter) gives a large perceived gain at zoom 1.3 and above with no memory risk. Chunked high-resolution repainting near the camera gives true close-up ground detail, but costs one to two weeks of OffscreenCanvas or worker work.

**Options to reject for now.** A third @3x set (216 ppu) would add about 1.7 GiB with mips just to help DPR-2 desktops at max zoom. Capping zoom there at about 2.8 achieves the same. FXAA and SMAA blur fine line detail, which is the wrong trade for hand-inked sprites with 1.5 to 2 px lines. WebGPU now ships in Safari 26 and Chrome Android 121+ ([web.dev](https://web.dev/blog/webgpu-supported-major-browsers)), but its gains are in draw-call-bound and compute work. ICS Media measured gains only at 10,000 draw calls ([ICS Media](https://ics.media/en/entry/230426/)), while this game's desktop budget is 28. A port would also mean rewriting every `onBeforeCompile` patch in TSL and keeping a WebGL2 fallback, whose render-target copies have known bugs ([three.js #31950](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/31950)). Live 3D re-rendering of models would need real-time edge detection and shadows to match the baked ink, which is weeks of work and a style-mismatch risk.

One open strategic question deserves a test rather than an opinion: should phones keep rendering at native DPR? Above zoom 2, a native Pixel 7 canvas holds more pixels than the atlas can fill. A cap at 2.0 would cut fragment work by about 42% on a 2.625 device, and with alpha-to-coverage the result might look nearly as sharp. The counter-argument is that DOM text is unaffected either way, while 3D edges and the ground at mid zoom genuinely gain from native. Only an on-device A/B comparison can settle it.

## A four-stage plan, from one-line fixes to compressed HD

Stages are ordered so that each one either costs nothing in memory or pays for the next. Effort assumes one developer who knows the codebase. Under the project rules, every change to `SPRITE_SOURCES` or packing needs `npm run bake` and committed `public/sprites*`. Visual changes also need their e2e screenshots re-baselined, and new browser checks belong in `e2e/` specs rather than one-off scripts.

| 1 | Alpha-to-coverage on object sprites | Hours, plus a screenshot test | **High**: smooth silhouettes and ink lines on High | Near zero on tilers | Only 5 coverage levels; no-op without MSAA; check the snow/season shader |
| 1 | Stop uploading icon pages to the GPU | ≤1 day | None | **−16 MiB (std) / −68 MiB (HD)** | Low; HUD must keep its image copies |
| 1 | Anisotropy 1 on sprites, 2 on ground | Hours | None to slight | Small fill saving | Low |
| 1 | Exact device-pixel canvas via `devicePixelContentBoxSize` | Hours | Unknown; removes a possible whole-frame resample at fractional DPR | None | Low; Chrome/Edge only |
| 1 | Correct stale docs (ARCHITECTURE "phone shows fewer pixels…", ROADMAP sizes) | Hours | None | None | None |
| 2 | Edge-colour bleed, ~8 px padding aligned to 4 px, better HD downscale filter | ~1 day, plus a repack | **Medium**: no fringes or dropouts when zoomed out | +2–4% atlas area; perhaps +1 draw call | e2e re-baseline; premultiplied alpha must agree with `alphaTest` and the shadow blend |

| 4 | Phone DPR cap ≈2.0 on High, if stage 0 shows parity | Hours | Neutral if alpha-to-coverage hides it | ≈−42% fragment work on a 2.625 phone | Softer mid-zoom ground and edges |
| 4 | Chunked high-res ground painting near the camera | 1–2 weeks | High at max zoom | A few 1024² chunk textures | Worker/OffscreenCanvas complexity; paint latency |
| – | Not recommended: @3x set, global `GROUND_PX` rise, WebGPU port, FXAA/SMAA, live 3D models | – | – | – | Cost far exceeds gain for this pipeline |

The tier rule for who gets what follows the same evidence. Today `pointer: coarse` picks standard and everything else picks HD. After stage 3 that can become: HD (or streamed HD) only when the compressed path is available and `navigator.deviceMemory` is at least 4 or unknown, standard otherwise, with the user's Sprite detail setting overriding it. `deviceMemory` is Chromium-only and bucketed ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Navigator/deviceMemory)), and detect-gpu's benchmark source stopped updating in December 2025 ([detect-gpu](https://github.com/pmndrs/detect-gpu)), so iPhones will still have to be judged by screen size and DPR.

## Seven measurements decide the uncertain rows

Every memory and sharpness figure above is arithmetic from texel counts and code constants. No GPU memory, frame time, transcode time or edge artefact has been measured on a device, and the browser's own copies (decoded images, context-restore buffers) are not counted. These seven measurements resolve the rows marked conditional:

| Measurement | How | Decides |
|---|---|---|
| Real memory at load and after 10 min, std vs HD, on a 3 GB iPhone and a Pixel 7 | Safari Web Inspector / Chrome `about:tracing` memory dumps; count reloads | Whether stage 1 savings are urgent; whether HD streaming is safe |
| Frame time at native vs DPR 2.0 vs 'auto', with and without alpha-to-coverage | e2e perf spec plus on-device runs at fixed zooms 1, 2, 3.6 | The phone DPR cap row and MSAA on Medium |
| Screenshot crops at zoom 0.33, 1, 3.6 on device, before and after stages 1–2 | Saved e2e screenshot specs, plus device captures | Fringe, dropout and edge-shimmer predictions |
| KTX2 trial encode of 2–3 pages (walls, units, buildings) with UASTC+RDO and ETC1S | `basisu`/`toktx` from the lossless bake cache | Download size per mode, alpha-cut drift, block artefacts on ink |
| Transcode and upload time per page on a mid-range Android | Timing in the loader | Whether streaming HD pages hitches |
| Cost of the CSS colour-grade filter on the canvas | Toggle `filter` on device with GPU profiling | Whether to fold the grade into a shader or RCAS pass |
| Canvas vs device-pixel match on a fractional-DPR phone | Compare `canvas.width` with `devicePixelContentBoxSize` | Whether the exact-canvas fix matters |

Two smaller unknowns remain open in the sources. It is undocumented whether mobile Chrome and Safari allocate a real multisample buffer for WebGL `antialias: true` or resolve on chip, and no maintained three.js CAS pass or published sprite-atlas KTX2 case study exists to copy.

## Conclusion

The research flips the obvious framing. "Higher resolution" on phones is mostly not about adding pixels to the atlas. At the zoom levels where most play happens, Hold the Hall's sprites are already minified, so what reads as soft is aliased cutout edges, fringed mips and a stretched canvas on the lower presets. Those are fixed by anti-aliasing the alpha cut and cleaning the atlas, at almost no memory cost. Resolution becomes the issue only when zoomed in, and there the constraint is GPU memory rather than art or download. That makes texture compression the hinge of the whole programme: without it, the game cannot even safely keep its current standard set on older iPhones, and with it, sharper units and HD-near-the-camera become affordable.

The second implication concerns how the game's look is described. The game's identity (ink outlines, painted ground, parchment fog) is resolution-tolerant by design, and the admired web games succeed the same way: they spend their budget on offline bakes and cheap ambient life, not texel density. The plan should keep that discipline. Each stage should earn its place by a measured gain on a real phone, and the "two identical sets at different resolutions" assumption should be retired, since the line widths already differ and a streamed, compressed, per-group atlas is where the pipeline is heading.
